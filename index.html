Project WHATNEY — NASA Mars to Table Challenge

Dr. Eline Oslo builds
resilience, not efficiency.

From a fjord-side lab in Stavanger, an aerospace engineer turned food scientist treats fermentation as flight telemetry — and Martian uncertainty as a feature worth designing for.

Stavanger, Norway · Food Science + Systems Engineering · Cat: Stockholm

The Premise

One system, two atmospheres.

A hydrogen UAV and a fermentation tank are, underneath, the same argument: a closed loop trying to stay alive inside conditions it cannot fully control. Eline's work began in aerospace — the unglamorous work of keeping something airborne when the wind doesn't read the flight plan. She moved it sideways into coffee and beer, where fermentation runs on the same currency — drift, noise, a system that has to hold together without a human hand steadying it constantly.

Resilience-as-a-Service is the name for that move. Not a product, not a slogan — a design stance. Every system Eline builds assumes the data will be messy, the reading will be wrong, the Martian afternoon will not match the simulation. The job isn't to eliminate that uncertainty. It's to build something that keeps working inside it.

The Force

Four ideas holding the saga together.

Uro

Non-Determinism

Eline stopped fearing uncertainty somewhere around her third failed batch. Martian environments don't hand you clean data — they hand you noise, drift, and gaps. She designs for the mess that's actually there, not the tidy dataset she wishes she had.

Observasjon

Observability-as-Infrastructure

Every fermenting tank is a black box with a flight recorder bolted on. Watching it closely, continuously, is not a report generated after the fact — it's the infrastructure itself.

Den Lille Seieren

The Small Win Philosophy

Martian infrastructure won't arrive as one grand system. It arrives as a hundred small, realistic wins — each one built to make the next person's job a little easier than hers was.

Terreng

Norwegian Context

Norway is Eline's imaginary Mars — a landscape of cold, pressure, and isolation that's close enough to test against and far enough to be honest about the gap. The Rusty Rover, her local pub, is where the whiteboard ideas get argued down to something that survives contact with reality.

A Scene

The Rusty Rover, past midnight.

The whiteboard behind the bar still has last week's diagram on it, half-erased, with a coffee ring where someone's mug sat too long. Stockholm is asleep on the stool that used to be reserved for regulars, unbothered by the argument currently happening over him.

"It's not that the reading's wrong," Eline says, tapping the dead spot on the chart. "It's that it's honest. The system needs to survive not knowing, for a few seconds, whether the tank is thriving or dying."

Someone across the table calls the whole idea Texas — too big, too loud, more ambition than the room can hold. Eline doesn't disagree. She just points out that Mars won't care how big the idea was. It'll only care whether the small, unglamorous version of it survives the trip.
