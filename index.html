Project ROMANA — NASA Mars to Table Challenge

Dr. Eline Oslo builds
resilience, not efficiency.

From a fjord-side lab in Stavanger, an aerospace engineer turned food scientist treats fermentation as flight telemetry — and Martian uncertainty as a feature worth designing for.

Stavanger, Norway · Food Science + Systems Engineering · Cat: Stockholm

The Premise

One system, two atmospheres.

A hydrogen UAV and a fermentation tank are, underneath, the same argument: a closed loop trying to stay alive inside conditions it cannot fully control. Eline's work began in aerospace — hydrogen fuel cells, flight controllers, the unglamorous math of keeping something airborne when the wind doesn't read the flight plan. She moved it sideways into coffee and beer, where fermentation runs on the same currency — temperature drift, chemical noise, a system that has to hold together without a human hand steadying it constantly.

Resilience-as-a-Service is the name for that move. Not a product, not a slogan — a design stance. Every system Eline builds assumes the data will be messy, the sensor will drop a reading, the Martian afternoon will not match the simulation. The job isn't to eliminate that uncertainty. It's to build something that keeps working inside it.

The Force

Four ideas holding the saga together.

Uro

Non-Determinism

Eline stopped fearing uncertainty somewhere around her third failed batch. Martian environments don't hand you clean data — they hand you noise, drift, and gaps. She designs for the mess that's actually there, not the tidy dataset she wishes she had.

Observasjon

Observability-as-Infrastructure

Every fermenting tank is a biological black box with a flight recorder bolted on. Temperature, pH, gas exchange, microbial load — ingested continuously, not sampled once and hoped for. The telemetry is the infrastructure, not a report generated after the fact.

Den Lille Seieren

The Small Win Philosophy

Martian infrastructure won't arrive as one grand system. It arrives as a hundred small, realistic wins — a sensor that survives a dust storm, a recipe that ferments correctly at the wrong pressure — each one built to make the next person's job a little easier than hers was.

Terreng

Norwegian Context

Norway is Eline's imaginary Mars — a landscape of cold, pressure, and isolation that's close enough to test against and far enough to be honest about the gap. The Rusty Rover, her local pub, is where the whiteboard ideas get argued down to something that survives contact with reality.

The Technical Bridge

Same console. Two atmospheres.

This is the argument made literal. Flip the switch — the dashboard doesn't change shape, only its labels. That's the point: a UAV's flight parameters and a fermentation tank's biological parameters are read by the same kind of instrument, asking the same kind of question — is this system still inside its survivable range?

Fuel cell stack temperature Fermentation vessel temperature 31.4 °C — nominal band
IMU drift / attitude noise pH drift / probe noise ± 0.03 — within tolerance
Battery reserve vs. fuel cell redundancy Yeast pitch rate vs. microbial redundancy 2 of 2 paths healthy
Telemetry packet loss Sensor dropout in cultivation tank 0.4% loss — logged, not alarmed
Wind gust / control surface correction CO₂ off-gas spike / valve correction auto-corrected in 1.2s

Reading the airframe.

Neither console is trying to hit a perfect number. Both are asking whether the system stays inside a survivable band while something it doesn't control — weather, wild yeast, a dust storm — pushes on it. Efficiency wants the tightest possible tolerance. Resilience wants the widest tolerance that still works. On Mars, that's the only trap worth avoiding.

What It's Built On

The ingest layer.

Underneath the saga is a real, containerized pipeline — the same kind of infrastructure a Martian outpost would need to trust its own data.

FastAPI the ingest surface — every reading enters through one disciplined door
Python the glue between sensor, model, and record
PostgreSQL / pgvector structured history, plus similarity search across every batch that came before
MinIO object storage for the raw, unglamorous telemetry nobody looks at until something goes wrong
Docker Compose the whole lab, reproducible on a laptop or a lander

A Scene

The Rusty Rover, past midnight.

The whiteboard behind the bar still has last week's diagram on it — a fuel cell stack, half-erased, with a coffee ring where someone's mug sat too long over the redundancy calculation. Stockholm is asleep on the stool that used to be reserved for regulars, unbothered by the argument currently happening over him.

"It's not that the sensor's wrong," Eline says, tapping the dead spot on the chart. "It's that it's honest. That's a real dropout. The system needs to survive not knowing, for four seconds, whether the tank is fermenting or dying."

Someone across the table calls the whole rig Texas — too big, too loud, more ambition than the room can hold. Eline doesn't disagree. She just points out that Mars won't care how big the idea was. It'll only care whether the small, unglamorous version of it survives the trip.
